Happy anniversary

My Darling Meriem,

Happy anniversary, my love.

I can't believe September 16 is back already. Looking back on everything we've shared, I just feel lucky.

I love your voice, I could listen to you talk for hours. I love your laugh, especially when I'm the reason for it. And more than anything, I love how you make a bad day feel smaller, just by talking to me.

My favourite memories aren't dramatic. They're the movie nights, the late-night conversations that somehow cover everything and nothing, until I fall asleep mid-sentence. Still sorry about that. Though that's kind of on you too, for making me feel safe enough to conk out mid-thought.

I love the little things about us. The jokes, the memories we keep stacking up, and obviously our stupid little line:

"I love you. A love only the branch of a willow tree could conjure."

Nobody else would get why that means what it means to us, and I like that. It's ours.

If I got to choose again, knowing everything I know now, I'd still choose you. Every time.

Here's to another year of us. More movies, more late nights, more laughing, and hopefully fewer sentences I fall asleep before finishing.

I love you, Meriem. More than I can put into words.

A love only the branch of a willow tree could conjure.

Always yours, ❤

All the Energy I Have

Chapter 1

The Weight of Fumes

There is a kind of tired that sleep does not fix. Raif knew it the way you know a room you have lived in too long, every corner of it, the exact place the light failed to reach. In the first weeks of college that autumn he was doing well and doing badly at the same time, and the strange thing was how neatly the two sat side by side. On paper he was fine. Better than fine. His grades came easily, the way things come to people who have learned to pour everything into the one place that will hold it. Coursework, deadlines, the small clean satisfaction of a problem solved. That part of him worked. That part of him always worked. The rest of it ran on fumes. He weighed a hundred kilograms then, though he did not say the number to anyone, did not think it so much as carry it, the way you carry a bag you have stopped noticing until you set it down and your shoulder aches for it. He had joined the gym and he was not taking it seriously, which was its own quiet joke he played on himself, the membership card in his wallet like a promise made to a stranger. He would go and he would half go through the motions and he would come home and the house would be the same and he would be the same, and the evening would open up in front of him, long and unlit.

It was the nights that were the worst. Not because anything happened in them, but because nothing did. He would lie there and his mind would go somewhere methodical and terrible, the way it went at everything, turning a private question over and over with the same cool competence he brought to his studies. The best way to go. The when of it. He never got further than the thinking. But he thought it, and the thinking was a country he visited more often than he would ever tell. The cruel part, the part that would have made a stranger laugh, was that people loved him. They did. They came to him. A friend would message at some odd hour, my girlfriend said this, what do I do, or I don't know if I should tell him how I feel, and Raif would sit up in the dark and give them the right answer, the wise one, the one that saw all the way around the problem. He was good at love from the outside. He could map anyone's heart but his own. He was the smart one, the nice one, the one everyone wanted to be around, and he would set his phone face down after and feel the silence come back into the room like water filling a footprint. He was very good at seeming fine. That was perhaps the truest thing about him then. He had made an art of it, the way you make an art of anything you practise every single day. Summer came, the summer of 2025, and summers when you are unravelling have a particular texture, all that loose bright time and nowhere to put yourself inside it. He made two friends online around then. Misha, and a boy called Callum who everyone called Cal. It was nothing, at first. It was the sort of thing you do when the days are too long, a couple of names in a group chat, jokes that land or do not, the small companionable noise of people you have never met and never expect to. He did not think it meant anything. Why would he. He had learned not to expect things to mean anything.

He kept a rock on his desk that summer. A grey one, ordinary, worn smooth, picked up on a beach in Wales on some trip he half remembered now, the wind off the water and the sound the stones made underfoot. He could not have told you why he kept it. It was just there, a small solid thing from a day the sea had been enormous and he had felt, briefly, like a person standing at the edge of something rather than inside it. He would pick it up sometimes without noticing, roll it in his palm while he thought, and set it down again. It was August, near the end, and he did not know yet that a friend two thousand kilometres away was about to ask him a question. He did not know that his life had a hinge in it, close now, and that he was already walking toward it in the dark without a light, the way he did everything. He only knew the nights were long, and that he was very tired, and that he had gotten so good at carrying it that he had almost forgotten it was heavy.

Chapter 2

The Nearest Object

It started with a question, the way these things sometimes do, from someone who had no idea what he was setting in motion. Cal asked it near the end of August, in the loose way men ask each other things online, half joking, half fishing. Raif, what's your type in a woman. Raif thought about it longer than the question probably deserved. He was good at this part, the part where other people's futures were laid out in front of him like a hand of cards he could read. He typed slowly. Amazing personality. Funny. Sweet. Someone who can talk about anything and has her own views on the world. A decent moral compass. Muslim, obviously. He paused, then added the last part honestly, because there was no reason to lie to Cal about it. Not too bothered about looks, though that would be a bonus. He hit send and expected nothing. What came back was three words that would, in a year, sound almost like prophecy. Raif, I have the perfect person for you. He remembers being suspicious more than anything. People said things like that. They meant well and delivered nothing. But Cal was insistent, and so a name appeared, and then a message window, and then a girl on the other side of two thousand kilometres who was clearly, immediately, terrified of him. Not of him exactly. Of being seen. He could tell within minutes. She wrote in short bursts and then apologised for them. She seemed convinced she was too much and not enough at the same time, worried about how she looked in a photo he hadn't asked to see, worried, he would learn later, about how her voice sounded, which was a strange thing to worry about, because her voice was the thing that would eventually undo him completely. He didn't know how to hold a conversation with someone that guarded. So he reached for a game he'd used before, a way of getting a person to say something true without noticing they were doing it. What's the closest object to you right now, he typed. Just whatever's nearest. A book, she wrote. He liked that. He liked that she didn't reach for something impressive, didn't perform. A book, full stop, no explanation. He imagined her sitting somewhere with it face-down beside her, a spine cracked open to hold her place. She asked him the same thing. What's yours. A rock, he told her. From a beach in Wales. He waited for her to laugh at him, or to move on. Instead she wanted to know about the rock, why he had it, what beach, whether it was smooth or sharp, and he found himself telling her about it, this ordinary grey thing he'd picked up on an ordinary grey day and carried home for reasons he'd never been able to explain to anyone. She didn't find it stupid. That was the first surprise. She found it interesting, and she said so, and something in the way she said it made him want to keep talking. So they kept talking. The first night ran late, and the second later, and after a week neither of them was pretending it was ordinary anymore. Ten in the evening became midnight, midnight became two, two became three and then four, the sky going from black to that thin bruised grey before dawn while they were still typing. He would look at the time and be genuinely shocked by it. Whole hours had gone somewhere he hadn't noticed, and he had nothing to show for them except a conversation he didn't want to end. They talked about everything. Fruits neither of them had ever tried. What they thought happened after you died. Films they loved and films they were embarrassed to admit they loved. She was funnier than he expected, funnier than she seemed to believe she was, saying something strange and brilliant and then immediately apologising for it as though she'd broken something. He got into the habit of not letting her apologise. Of just answering the strange thing as though it were the most natural thing in the world to say, which, coming from her, it was. The gym in the mornings started to feel different. Not easier, but pointed at something. He would drag himself out of bed on two hours of sleep, his body still heavy with the weight he was only just beginning to lose, and there would be a message waiting from the night before, or the night still going, and he'd read it standing in his kitchen and feel, for the first time in a long time, that the day had a reason to happen. They were still just friends then. He kept telling himself that, kept it clearly labelled, because the labelling felt safer than whatever was actually going on.

But he was already staying awake for her. That was the thing he couldn't argue his way out of. Across two thousand kilometres, in the small dead hours when he used to be at his worst, he was awake, and glad of it, and thinking about a girl and a book and a rock from a beach in Wales.

Chapter 3

How Much You Want to Bet

There is a particular arithmetic to falling for someone across two thousand kilometres. You count the hours the way a miser counts coins. You learn that ten at night is a doorway, that one and two and three in the morning are rooms you did not know your life contained. Raif had spent the summer running on fumes, and now he lay awake at three because he wanted to, which was a different thing entirely, and his eyes stung, and he did not care. He noticed the small things because the small things were all he had of her. She forgot what she had said an hour ago and then argued that she had never said it. She said "mhm" the way other people signed their names, and it meant yes, and it meant I am listening, and sometimes it meant you are being ridiculous and I am too kind to say so. When he told her about something he had done, some plan, some ambition, she would say "as you should," flat and certain, as though his winning were simply a matter of record. She hated men. She said it plainly, the way you might report the weather. Raif found this funny in a way he could not fully explain to himself. He was a man. He was, he supposed, on the list. And yet she stayed up until four talking to him about the origin of fruit and whether cats understood affection, and he understood that being the exception to her contempt was worth more than being anyone else's rule.

It took him two weeks to say it. Two weeks of drafting sentences in his head at the gym, on the walk home, in the dark before sleep. He was, everyone had always said, the one people came to. The one who knew what to say. And here he was, wordless, clicking his fingers at the ceiling like the word he wanted was hovering just out of reach. So on the sixteenth of September he did it the way a coward does a brave thing, sideways, so he could pretend it was a joke if it went wrong. I have feelings for this girl, he wrote, and I don't know how to tell her. We're really good friends and I don't want to mess the dynamic up if she says no. How much you want to bet she says no. Everything in your savings account, she wrote back. He read it twice. There was something in the speed of her reply, the wit of it, that made his hands cold. Your highness, he typed, I was referring to you. Then nothing. The nothing lasted ten minutes, which is not a long time unless you are inside it, in which case it is a geological age. He got up. He sat down. He read the message he had sent and hated every letter of it. He rang Adam, who answered the way friends do at strange hours, and Raif talked about anything, football, college, the weather, while his eyes never left the screen. Adam, he would think later, had no idea he was being used as a life raft. I feel the same for you. Four words. He said something to Adam that made no sense and hung up and lay very still, the way you lie still when you are afraid a good thing might notice you and think better of it. He had spent nights that summer working out the best way to leave. Now he was working out how to stay awake long enough to keep talking to her. After that the nicknames began, tentative at first, then like a second vocabulary they were both fluent in without study. He tried "Darling" and she let it stand. "Her Highness," because of the bet, because of the way she carried herself even shy, even insecure about her voice and her face, neither of which he could see and both of which he already loved without evidence. "Sweetheart." "Sweetie." She gave back "lovie" and "bbg," and he pretended, out loud, to be appalled, and privately turned the words over like the rock from the Welsh beach, the one he had told her about that first night, the one still on his shelf. She would say the strangest thing, some observation nobody else would have thought to make, and then apologise for it immediately, as though honesty were a mess she had tracked in on her shoes. He wanted to tell her not to apologise. He wanted to tell her that the strange things were the whole point, that a life without them was the life he had nearly ended. He did not say any of that. Not yet. He said goodnight when the sky was already grey, and he lay there thinking the thought he would think a hundred times after. I can't wait to have these conversations when I'm right next to her. Two thousand kilometres away, choosing her, and choosing her again in the morning.

Chapter 4

The Willow and the Corner

Thursday was theirs before either of them decided it should be. It started the way most of their habits started, without ceremony. A film queued up, the two of them in separate rooms two thousand kilometres apart, watching the same frame at the same second because they counted down out loud. Three. Two. One. Play. The first was Wall-E. He remembers this the way people remember the exact spot they were standing when something in them tilted. A small robot alone on a dead planet, collecting things nobody wanted, and Meriem going quiet in a way that made him go quiet too. When it ended neither of them said much. He just knew he wanted to do it again the next week, and the week after that, until the weeks became a thing with a shape. They were not always good at protecting the day. Life got in the way, hers or his, and the ritual bent to fit. If Thursday failed, they took the next possible night. This was the rule, unspoken and iron. The film could move. The having of it could not. He would tell you the low points as easily as the high ones. Interstellar, for instance, where he fought sleep like a man fighting the tide, and lost, and she never let him forget it. Ten Things I Hate About You, which she loved and which he pretended to be neutral about and then quoted for a week. Anime films that made her cry. A romance she called predictable ten minutes in and watched anyway. And then Obsession, the horror, the one with the willow tree, which gave them the sentence they now used like a password. I love you so much, he would say, the love only the branch of a willow tree could conjure. He knew exactly how absurd it sounded. That was the point. Somewhere in the ridiculousness of it there was something he could not have said plainly, and she understood that, and let him hide the enormous thing inside the silly thing. There was a companion piece to it, darker and funnier, where he would announce he would watch her sleep from the corner of the room, and she would tell him he was unhinged, and he would agree, and neither of them meant a word except the one underneath, which was: I do not want to be anywhere you are not. There was a song too. Energy, by Johnny Gunny. There is a line in it about giving all your energy and getting all of someone back, might as well just be in love. They deployed it like a dare. He would send it, or half-sing it, and she would roll her eyes in that way he could hear through text, and it was a joke and it was also the truest thing he could offer her. Give me everything. Here is everything. The nicknames arrived and multiplied and refused to leave. He had four he loved above all others. Darling. Her Highness. Sweetheart. Sweetie. She had her own arsenal. Honey. Handsome. His Highness. His Majesty. And baby girl, which she used precisely because it made him pretend to object, and which he had long since stopped pretending about. Lovie. Bbg. There was a whole grammar to it, a small kingdom of two with its own titles. She said things. Strange things, funny things, things that arrived out of nowhere at four in the morning, and then she apologised for them a half-second later as though the words had escaped without permission. She was brutally honest in a way most people never risked being, and it came out of her before she could soften it, and then came the apology, chasing it down the hall. He collected these. Her mhm. Her as you should. The way she declared, with total conviction, that she hated men, all of them, an entire gender, and how he found this the most endearing thing about her, because he was a man, and she had made him the exception without ever quite admitting it. He had his own tells. The finger-clicking when a word had slipped out of reach and he was trying to summon it back. The Muhahahaha he produced whenever he thought he was being funny, which was often, and which she tolerated with the patience of a saint or a woman deeply in love, or both. And then there were question nights, older than the relationship itself. They had done them as friends, back when everything was still a game of getting closer without saying so. Any question in the world, answered as honestly as it could be answered. The origin of fruits at four in the morning. Whether their views had shifted since the last time they asked. Who they were becoming, tested gently against who they had been. This was the texture of them. Not one grand thing. A thousand small ones, private, portable, built to survive the distance.

Chapter 5

One Phrase

March came in grey and stayed that way. It started as an ordinary day, which is the thing about the days that undo you. Raif messaged in the morning the way he always did. Nothing. He messaged again at lunch, some small thing, a photo, a question about her day. Nothing. He watched the screen the way you watch a kettle, telling himself it meant nothing, that she was busy, that she'd forgotten her phone somewhere the way she forgot things, endearingly, all the time. Then one message arrived, and it was three words. Let's break up. After that, nothing again. He read it standing in the kitchen. He read it the way you read a word you don't recognise, expecting it to rearrange itself into something that made sense. He asked her what was wrong. He asked her what he'd done, whether he could help, whether she'd just talk to him. He sent these into the quiet and the quiet took them all and gave nothing back. There is a particular cruelty in caring for someone who has gone silent. You keep offering your hands to a door that won't open. After a while Raif stopped. Fine, he thought. I'm not going to sit here bleeding for someone who won't even look at me. He put the phone face down and went to the gym, because moving was better than not moving, because the barbell at least gave him back exactly what he put into it. He was walking home when it buzzed. The streetlights were coming on, that thin orange that makes everything look further away than it is. Hey. Are you ignoring me. Have you finished at the gym. Are you going home. Yep, he wrote. Going home now. I'll let you know when I'm back. Dry. Casual. He was proud of how flat he could make the words when his chest was anything but. Are you annoyed with me. He looked at that for a long time. He didn't have it in him to explain. No, he wrote. I'm not annoyed. Then, because he needed to know, because it was the only question that actually mattered under all the others: Do you still love me. The reply took two hours. He walked home in that time. He ate. He didn't taste it. When it came it wasn't an answer at all. It's so sad how one phrase can affect a person's life that greatly. And something in him gave way. Not dramatically. Quietly, the way a shelf holds and holds and then simply lets go. He typed everything he had been swallowing for months. That he was sick of feeling like he had to be smaller when she wasn't okay. That he couldn't keep being the only one holding the thing up. I'm done, he wrote. Don't message me. Don't call me. We're through.

Then he turned the phone off, and the flat was very loud with him not saying anything. She kept trying. He watched the missed calls stack up over three days and did not answer a single one. It was not pride, exactly. It was the exhausted certainty that if he picked up too soon he would forgive her before either of them understood what they were forgiving. So he waited. She asked, again and again, for a phone call. On the third day he wrote back: Fine. Let's talk. Tell me whatever you need to tell me. Her voice on the line was the same voice that read him to sleep, only smaller. She said she'd been stupid. She said she knew it, that the words had come out of somewhere frightened and she'd let them sit there like a wound she was too proud to dress. She said she wouldn't do it again. He believed her, mostly because he wanted to, and because underneath the anger the thing he actually felt was relief so total it made his eyes sting. They rebuilt. Then, a week later, it was Raif who nearly broke it. The distance had been sitting on his chest, two thousand kilometres of it, and one night he decided out loud that it couldn't work, and he ended it. For a day, maybe two, he lived inside that decision and hated it. He turned it over. Either I waste time and it fails, he thought, or it could have worked and I'm the fool who threw it away and gets to be miserable about that forever. He knew which regret he could live with. He asked for another chance, ashamed of himself, and she gave it. She just gave it. There were smaller storms after. A woman he'd counted as a friend, a situation he could have avoided with a little more sense, sorted out with honesty and a great many apologies. But the lesson had already landed by then, and it was a plain one. Wait before you decide. Say the thing before it becomes the wrong thing. Trust each other, and be careful with everyone else.

They came out the other side quieter, and closer, and still there.

Chapter 6

The Weight of a Voice

Here is what she is to me now. She is the last voice I hear most nights. She reads to me, or I read to her, and somewhere in the middle of a chapter one of us goes under. Her voice does something to me I still cannot explain. It is low and steady and it moves like water over a page, and within minutes I am gone. She thinks she is boring me. She is not. She is the only thing that has ever made the noise in my head go quiet. She still thinks I get annoyed when she goes for a nap. I have told her a hundred times that I do not. She says mhm and closes her eyes anyway, half certain I am sulking on the other side of the screen, two thousand kilometres away, when really I am just sitting there glad she feels safe enough to sleep while I am there. I let her believe I mind a little. It gives her something to apologise for later, and I like the shape of her apologies. She is brutally honest and then instantly sorry for it. She will say something so blunt it knocks the air out of me, some observation about my face or my grammar or the way I overthink everything, and before I have even finished laughing she has already said sorry, oh my god, sorry, I did not mean it like that. She did mean it like that. That is what I love. She does not soften the world to make it easier to hold. She hands it to me exactly as it is and then worries she has hurt me.

She talks to cats like they are old friends who owe her money. She talks to babies in a voice I have no name for, and I go still and just listen, because there is a version of her in that voice that the rest of the world does not get to see. She hates men. She says it with total conviction, and then she calls me lovie, or bbg, which I pretend to hate and do not hate at all. I am the exception she keeps having to explain to herself. As you should, she says, when I do something right. It is the highest praise I know. I am not the person I was before her. I want to say this plainly because she knew that person and chose to stay anyway. I weighed a hundred kilos once. I am twenty-five lighter now. There were nights, before her, when I was working out the best way to leave, and I do not say that for pity, I say it because she should know what her ordinary presence has done without ever being told it was doing anything. I can be in photographs now. Not comfortably, not always, but I can stand in front of a camera and let it happen. She did not fix me. She just kept staying, and staying is its own kind of medicine. When I sat my English exam she shouted at me for the better part of two months. Revise. Have you revised. Why are you talking to me, go and revise. She is stubborn about this. She hates men, hates being helped, wants to build every single thing with her own two hands, and yet she spent all that time trying to build something in me. When the pass came through I felt it belonged to both of us, which she would deny, which is exactly why I love telling her. We are settled now, in a way we were not before. The recent months have been quiet in the good sense. No wars. Small ones, when I annoy her, which I do, because it is apparently my one flaw and everyone including my mother agrees. I click my fingers when I am chasing a word that has slipped away from me. I say Muhahahaha when I think I am being funny, and she rolls her eyes, and that eye-roll is a room I would happily live in.

Last week we spent an hour on fruit. Where mangoes come from. What she has never tasted. Whether a tomato counts. It was four in the morning her time and neither of us would let the other sleep first. This is what we are. Two people who would rather be tired together than rested apart. Two people who talk about the origin of fruit at hours no sane person should be awake, across a distance that should make all of this impossible. She is the funniest person I know and the kindest, and she does not fully believe either of those things about herself. So let this be the honest answer to one of our question nights, the truest one I have. What are you to me. You are the reason the nights stopped being something to survive.

Chapter 7

Two Thousand Kilometres and Then None

There is a map he pulls up sometimes, late, when the flat has gone quiet and the only light is the one from his screen. He puts his finger on where he is and drags it across to where she is, and the line the map draws for him is always the same distance, two thousand kilometres, give or take, a number that used to feel like a sentence and now feels like a countdown. He does not tell her he does this. It would sound like the kind of thing that gets a laugh and then an apology from her, one of those quick sweet apologies she throws out before she has even finished being funny. But he does it. He measures the space, and then he closes the map, because the point of measuring a distance is knowing it can be crossed. They talk about it more now, the after. The place they will live. Whether there will be a cat, which there will be, because she has already decided, and when Meriem decides something about a cat there is no vote to be had. He has heard her talk to cats. He has heard the whole register of her drop into something soft and ridiculous and completely serious, a voice she keeps for animals and babies and, occasionally, when she forgets to guard it, for him. He wants to be in a room where that voice is not coming out of a speaker. He wants to hear it fill actual air. It is a Thursday. He has the movie queued already, has had it queued since the afternoon, the way a person keeps a small good thing in their pocket to touch. He does not remember what it is. It does not especially matter. What matters is the ritual of it, the two of them settling in from either end of the line, the small negotiations about who is more tired, her insisting she is fine and then falling asleep twenty minutes in, him lowering his voice without meaning to, following the film alone so she does not have to. He has made peace with being the one who stays awake. Someone has to guard the corner of the room. Almost a year. September the sixteenth is coming round again, the date sitting on the calendar like a door he already knows the shape of. He thinks about the version of himself who existed before that date, twenty-five kilograms heavier and quieter in all the ways that mattered, the boy everyone came to for advice who had no advice for himself, who used to lie awake doing a different kind of arithmetic entirely. He does not go back there often. But he goes back far enough to be grateful, which is a distance too, and one worth crossing now and then. "You've gone quiet," she says. "Thinking." "Clicking your fingers?" "No." He is. He stops. "Mhm," she says, and he can hear the smile in it, the flat little verdict she hands down when she has caught him and decided to let him off. As you should, he thinks, before she can say it. As you should. He has been trying to say something for a few minutes, the way he was trying to say something two Septembers ago, sideways, in the wrong words, terrified. He is better at it now. Not perfect. But better. He does not need Adam on the other line anymore, does not need a bet to hide behind. "You know that song," he says. "Which song." "Ours. The Gunny one." There is a pause, but it is not the two-hour pause. It is the good kind, the kind where he can hear her thinking. Then, quietly, she gives him the line back, the one about energy, about giving all of yourself, about how you may as well just be in love at that point. She says it like a joke, because that is how they have always said it. But underneath the joke is the thing the joke has been protecting all along, which is that it was never really a joke. "Give me all your energy," he says. "I'll give you all of me," she says, and this time neither of them laughs. The film keeps playing. Somewhere two thousand kilometres away she is settling deeper into her pillow, and he can hear her breathing start to lengthen and slow, the way it does, the way that means he has kept her up too late again and would do it every night if she let him. He turns the volume down. He watches the corner of a room he is not yet in. Next Thursday there will be another one. And the one after. And then, sooner than the map wants him to believe, there will be a Thursday with no screen between them at all, and he will finally get to find out what her voice does to the actual air of an actual room. He can wait for that. He is good at staying up.
